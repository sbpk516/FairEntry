BEHIND THE NUMBER
A SHORT GUIDE

What the page is telling you

Condition and direction are different

A reading can still be healthy compared with history while moving in the wrong direction. Each card shows both its current condition and its change over two years. A two-year change compares endpoints, not every movement in between.

Colors have a specific meaning

Supportive on this measure Typical or worth watching Adverse or elevated Context or missing data

Typical ranges are the middle half of historical readings. Their boundaries are descriptive conventions, not proven danger thresholds. Inflation colors measure the historical size of its gap from 2%; the Fed’s formal 2% target applies to headline PCE. Interest rates have no fixed ideal, so they receive a neutral status.

The headline is a summary, not a risk score

The briefing uses four measures: GDP growth, unemployment, headline inflation and financial conditions. “Several signs of strain” means at least three have an adverse classification; “Mostly supportive readings” means all four have a supportive classification. Otherwise the picture is mixed. Any missing or stale core measure makes the picture incomplete. These are editorial display rules, not a recession model. Valuations and overlapping yield curves are not added up into a crash score.

The evidence is always one click away

Choose “Why this status?” on any card for the exact thresholds, observation dates, full history, original sources and CSV download. Economic data comes from FRED and its originating agencies. Valuation sources are Federal Reserve/BEA data and Robert Shiller’s dataset. History can be revised.

A couple of useful translations

Percentage points: a change from 4% to 5% is one percentage point. Annualized growth: the quarterly pace expressed as if it continued for a year. Historical midpoint: the median, with half of observations on either side. Inverted curve: short-term bond yields are higher than long-term yields.
